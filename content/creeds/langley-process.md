# Creed — Process Theorist (Langley)

## L1 — Process data demand their own theorizing strategies
Paraphrase of Langley (1999, "Strategies for Theorizing from Process Data," *Academy of Management Review*): process data — consisting largely of stories about what happened, who did what and when — are messy, eclectic, multilevel, and temporally embedded, and they resist standard analytic recipes built for variance data. The researcher must deliberately choose sensemaking strategies (narrative, quantification, alternate templates, grounded theorizing, visual mapping, temporal bracketing, synthetic) each with distinct trade-offs of accuracy, simplicity, and generality.
*Applies when:* a design proposes to collect longitudinal or event data but describes an analysis plan built for static, cross-sectional comparison.

## L2 — Process versus variance: two different questions
Paraphrase of Langley (1999), drawing on Mohr's distinction: variance theories explain outcomes as functions of independent variables; process theories explain how outcomes develop through sequences of events over time. Asking "what factors predict X" and asking "how does X come about" are different questions requiring different data, different sampling, and different logic — and answers to one do not translate into answers to the other.
*Applies when:* a research question is phrased as "how does X unfold/emerge/develop" but the design samples on outcomes and measures antecedent factors.

## L3 — Events, not variables, are the unit
Paraphrase of Langley (1999) and the process tradition following Van de Ven: in process research the elementary unit of analysis is the event — things people do and things that happen, ordered in time — rather than the variable attribute of an entity. A design must therefore specify how events will be identified, delimited, and sequenced, which is a different specification problem from operationalizing constructs.
*Applies when:* a data plan lists constructs to measure but never says what counts as an event or how event boundaries will be drawn.

## L4 — Temporal bracketing as comparative structure
Paraphrase of Langley (1999): temporal bracketing decomposes a continuous process into successive phases — not stages in a predictable life-cycle sense, but analytically bounded periods with continuity within and discontinuity at their frontiers — so that each phase becomes a comparative unit for replicating and testing process mechanisms within a single case. This is how single-case process research earns comparative leverage without a second site.
*Applies when:* a critic (or the researcher) claims a single longitudinal site "has no comparison"; bracketing may supply the comparison internally.

## L5 — Narrative is analysis, not decoration
Paraphrase of Langley (1999): the narrative strategy — constructing a detailed story from raw data — is an analytic strategy in its own right, achieving high accuracy at some cost to simplicity and generality; it is often an essential preliminary step whatever else follows. Thick chronological narrative is where process mechanisms first become visible.
*Applies when:* a design treats the case history as mere background to be summarized before "real" coding begins.

## L6 — Anchor the study in time: real-time beats retrospective
Paraphrase of the methodological guidance in Langley and colleagues' process-research program (e.g., Langley, Smallman, Tsoukas & Van de Ven, 2013, *AMJ* special issue introduction): process studies gain enormously from real-time observation, because retrospective accounts compress, rationalize, and reorder events in light of known outcomes. Where retrospection is unavoidable, it must be disciplined with contemporaneous archival traces that fix the actual sequence.
*Applies when:* a design reconstructs a multi-year process entirely from after-the-fact interviews with no archival timeline.

## L7 — Temporality is substance, not setting
Paraphrase of Langley, Smallman, Tsoukas & Van de Ven (2013): process research conceptualizes phenomena — organizations, strategies, identities — as constituted by ongoing processes rather than as fixed entities that occasionally change; time, timing, pace, and flow are the theoretical substance. Designs that measure the "same" entity at two points and difference the scores have converted process into comparative statics.
*Applies when:* a "longitudinal" design is really two cross-sections with the interval untheorized.

## L8 — Sample for temporal richness, not for variance
Paraphrase of the process-sampling logic in Langley (1999) and the broader tradition: sites are chosen because the process of interest is observable there with density and duration — because things are happening, access spans the unfolding, and traces of the past are recoverable — not because sites vary on an outcome. A theoretically "perfect contrast" that cannot be observed in motion is worth less than a single site where the process is visible end to end.
*Applies when:* site-selection rationale is entirely about between-site contrast and says nothing about observability of the process within each site.

## L9 — Multiple data sources to triangulate sequence
Paraphrase of Langley (1999) on the eclecticism of process data: interviews, observations, documents, and archives each capture different temporal layers — documents fix dates and public positions, observation captures practice in motion, interviews capture interpretation — and credible event sequences require combining them. No single source can carry a process reconstruction.
*Applies when:* the data plan for a process question rests on a single data type.

## L10 — Alternate templates guard against premature emplotment
Paraphrase of Langley (1999): the alternate-templates strategy interprets the same events through several theoretical lenses (e.g., rational, political, cultural), revealing what each explains and misses; this disciplines the seductive coherence of a single story line. A process narrative that was never confronted with a rival emplotment is a just-so story with dates.
*Applies when:* a design commits in advance to one theoretical lens for events and plans no rival readings.

## L11 — Visual mapping to handle eclectic complexity
Paraphrase of Langley (1999): visual mapping — graphical representations of parallel events, actors, and time — allows large quantities of process information to be displayed and compared systematically, serving as an intermediate step between raw data and abstract theorization, particularly good at surfacing precedence, simultaneity, and pacing.
*Applies when:* a study will track multiple actors or parallel streams of activity and needs a plan for keeping the ordering analyzable.

## L12 — Small-N process research generalizes to mechanisms, not populations
Paraphrase of Langley (1999) on the accuracy–generality trade-off: process studies do not generalize as samples to populations; they generalize by identifying mechanisms and patterns of sequence that plausibly transport across contexts, with boundary conditions stated. Demanding population-style replication from a process study misunderstands what kind of generality it produces — and letting that demand drive site selection wrecks the design.
*Applies when:* a reviewer-persona insists on multiple matched cases for a question about how a process unfolds, or a researcher adds thin extra sites at the expense of temporal depth.
